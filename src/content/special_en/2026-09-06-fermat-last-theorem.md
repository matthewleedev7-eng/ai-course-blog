## What We Witnessed: The Future of Mathematics Has Changed

In September 2026, Anthropic's Claude completely solved a 358-year-old mathematical mystery.

Fermat's Last Theorem. A problem Pierre de Fermat left in a book's margin in 1637:

> "For any integer n greater than 2, there are no three positive integers x, y, and z that satisfy x^n + y^n = z^n."

This deceptively simple statement survived 358 years. When Andrew Wiles finally proved it in 1995, the scientific community erupted. His proof was revolutionary, beautiful, and **one of humanity's greatest intellectual achievements.**

Then in 2026, AI proved it in 11 days using a completely different method.

**What's the difference?**

---

## The Definition of "Proof" Has Changed

Wiles' proof: About 100 pages. Based on human trust. Verified by colleagues, approved by the scientific community. **Comprehensible to humans.**

Claude's proof: 13 million lines of code. Computer-verified at each line. No mathematician can fully grasp it. **But there's no room for doubt.**

Here's the key:

**The meaning of "proof" has fundamentally shifted.**

- **Then**: Proof = text that humans understand, colleagues verify, and the scientific community publishes
- **Now**: Proof = formal verification that computers automatically confirm at each step

Previously, **"belief"** was necessary. How many mathematicians truly understood Wiles' entire proof? Most simply trusted that peer mathematicians had verified it.

Now, **"certification"** is necessary. The computer's certification that every line is verified.

**What humans must do:**

## The Truth About AI's Acceleration: Exponential Growth Isn't Linear

Claude's achievement doesn't simply mean "computation is faster."

**What we've missed:**

The difference isn't **scale**; it's a **qualitative shift**.

- Olympiad problems: Multiple choice, fixed answer length, simple verification
- Fermat's Last Theorem: Open-ended, proof length undefined, verification extremely complex

AI can now solve **unstructured problems.**

What does this mean?

**The next frontier is science itself.**

Unsolved problems in physics. Protein folding in biology. Systems analysis in economics. All of this is now within AI's reach.

We expected AI's **linear evolution**. Reality is **exponential change.**

## The True Insight We Gain from Mathematical Problems

Why did Fermat's Last Theorem survive 358 years?

Not simply because it was hard. **Because we lacked the right tools.**

- Fermat's era (1600s): Elementary mathematics couldn't solve it
- Euler, Lagrange era (1700s-1800s): Better tools developed, but still insufficient
- Modular forms discovered (1950s-1980s): Tools finally became adequate
- Wiles (1995): Masterfully deployed tools to complete the proof
- Claude (2026): New tool (formal verification) enables an entirely different proof method

**Do you see the pattern?**

In each era, unsolved problems remain until they can be solved. But **when they are solved, it signals the completion of that era's tools and methodology.**

Fermat's Last Theorem was never just a "math problem." It was **the question: "Do we understand mathematics well enough?"**

358 years later, we have an answer:

"We still don't. But now computers can prove things in ways we cannot."
